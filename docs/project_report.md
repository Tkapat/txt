## Abstract

Generative language models can now produce fluent product reviews and long documents at very low cost. The practical question for a reader, a marketplace, or a researcher is no longer only "is this review fake?" but also "was this text written by a machine?" Those are related but different questions: a review can be AI-written and truthful, and a human-written review can be deceptive. Existing fake-review work mostly studies short, English, single-generator data and reports in-distribution accuracy. It rarely tests whether a detector survives an unseen generator or an unseen domain, and it rarely ships as a usable, calibrated, privacy-preserving tool.

This project builds **txt**, an end-to-end AI-writing detector for two granularities: short reviews (15–160 words) and long documents (up to 100,000 characters). Two transformer classifiers are trained: a **DeBERTa-v3-base** review detector and a **RoBERTa-base** document detector initialised from an HC3-trained checkpoint. The review model is trained on pre-ChatGPT Amazon reviews plus synthetic reviews generated by several open-weight LLM families; the document model is trained on HC3 and RAID with held-out generators (`gpt4`, `cohere-chat`, `mpt-chat`) and a held-out domain (`recipes`). Splits are grouped by product and by document so that chunks or products never leak across train, validation, and test. Thresholds are fitted on human validation text to control the false-positive rate, and the document model reports an unseen-generator sensitivity of 0.985 and a specificity of 0.980.

The models run locally in **fp32 PyTorch** behind a small **FastAPI** service, called by a **Next.js 16** application. The report documents dynamic INT8 quantization in detail, including the negative result on the review model (all scores collapsed to about 0.73, destroying separation) and the AUC-gated acceptance rule used for the document model. The product supports pasted text, text-based PDF/DOCX/TXT/MD files, and bulk review lists/CSV, with evidence highlights, chunk-level scores, an error-corrected prevalence estimate, and an explicit "estimate, not proof" disclaimer.

**Keywords:** AI-generated text detection, fake review detection, DeBERTa-v3, RoBERTa, HC3, RAID, Amazon Reviews 2023, quantization, calibration, Next.js, FastAPI, PyTorch.

## 1. The Gap We Cover

### 1.1 What existing work already does

The fake-review literature is rich but concentrated in a few areas:

- **Classical machine learning on hand-crafted features.** SVM, logistic regression, Naïve Bayes, random forests, and gradient boosting on TF-IDF, n-grams, sentiment, and linguistic features are widely used and often report 85–96% accuracy on a single dataset [4], [8], [12], [13], [14]. These models are cheap and interpretable, but they depend heavily on surface cues and tend to degrade when the generator or domain changes.
- **Deep learning on short reviews.** CNN, LSTM, GRU, and BiLSTM architectures over one-hot, Word2Vec, or TF-IDF representations are common [16]. Reported numbers are high (for example, BiLSTM + Word2Vec at 98.46% accuracy in [16]), but the evaluation is usually an 80:20 random split of one corpus, which can overstate real-world performance.
- **Transformer-based detectors.** DistilBERT, BERT, RoBERTa, and XLNet fine-tunes are strong baselines for ChatGPT-rephrased and AI-generated reviews [17], [7]. Explainability work adds LIME, SHAP, or layer-wise relevance propagation [7], [17].
- **Domain- and language-specific studies.** Work on Dravidian languages [1], tourism/hospitality [2], and fuzzy-CNN models on Amazon data [11] shows that performance is sensitive to language, domain, and review style.
- **Surveys and benchmark critiques.** Recent surveys [5], [6], [10], [15] highlight recurring problems: limited labelled data, inconsistent label construction, data leakage, concept drift as generators improve, weak cross-domain transfer, and a lack of trustworthy, uncertainty-aware evaluation.
- **Generation-side research.** Work such as MidGAN [18] shows how quickly generated text becomes more diverse and human-like, which is exactly why detectors must be tested against generators they were not trained on.

### 1.2 The gap

The gap this project targets is not "another classifier with a higher accuracy on one dataset." It is a set of connected gaps that matter in deployment:

1. **AI authorship is treated as a proxy for fakeness.** Most work labels reviews as genuine vs. fake/deceptive, often using human-written spam (e.g., OpSpam) or a single generator. That conflates *intent* with *authorship*. A detector that says "this reads as AI-written" is a different, useful signal, and it should not be presented as proof that the content is false. txt keeps this distinction explicit in its labels, wording, and warnings.
2. **Generalisation to unseen generators and domains is rarely measured.** Random splits let chunks of the same document, or reviews of the same product, appear in both training and test sets. The result is optimistic. This project uses grouped splits by `parent_asin` and `doc_id`, holds out entire generators and a domain, and reports the unseen-generator number rather than only the in-distribution number.
3. **Long-form text is under-served.** Fake-review papers focus on short reviews. A marketplace also needs to inspect long documents (listings, articles, reports, PDFs). A 256-token classifier cannot score a 20-page document directly; it needs chunking, aggregation, and chunk-level explanations. txt provides a second model and a document pipeline for this.
4. **Calibration and honest uncertainty are missing.** A probability is not useful if the threshold is arbitrary. The training pipeline fits thresholds on human validation text to bound the false-positive rate, records sensitivity/specificity, and uses an error-corrected prevalence estimate (Rogan–Gladen) for bulk review reports.
5. **Research prototypes rarely become usable tools.** Many papers stop at a notebook and a table. txt is an end-to-end product with a browser UI, local inference, file extraction, bulk analysis, evidence highlighting, rate limits, typed API errors, and no database.
6. **CPU deployment and quantization trade-offs are not documented.** Practitioners need to know whether INT8 is safe for a probability-emitting detector. This project documents a clear negative result on the review model and an AUC-gated acceptance procedure on the document model, then ships fp32.

### 1.3 Why this is useful

- **For consumers:** a second opinion on a review or a long product description, with the exact phrases that pushed the score, and a visible reminder that the output is an estimate.
- **For platforms and moderators:** a local, bulk triage tool for review queues. Moderators can sort by AI probability, inspect flagged reviews, and see whether many reviews share a template (group signals) without sending user text to a third-party API.
- **For researchers:** a reproducible two-model baseline with grouped splits, held-out generators, calibration metadata, and a documented quantization study that can be extended.
- **For privacy-sensitive users:** the models run on the local machine/CPU behind a local service. There is no database and no request-body logging; text is processed in memory and discarded.
- **For the "AI-written vs. fake" debate:** the product consistently says "likely AI-written", never "fake", and returns a `detector_not_proof` warning. This is a small but important contribution to responsible deployment.

### 1.4 Contributions

1. A **two-granularity** detector: a short-review model and a long-document model behind one routing rule.
2. A **generalisation-oriented training protocol**: grouped splits, held-out generators, a held-out domain, leakage checks, and unseen-generator reporting.
3. A **calibrated deployment**: thresholds from human validation data, sensitivity/specificity metadata, and an error-corrected prevalence estimate for bulk reviews.
4. A **quantization study** with a reproducible acceptance rule and a clear recommendation (fp32 for this product).
5. A **working product**: Next.js UI, typed API, file extractors, bulk reports, evidence, rate limits, and local inference.

## 2. Model Training

This is the core technical section. It explains the label semantics, the two datasets, the splits, the fine-tuning settings, the calibration, and the artifacts that were actually shipped.

### 2.1 Label semantics and evaluation protocol

Both models use the same fixed label order:

```text
0 = human
1 = ai
id2label = {0: "human", 1: "ai"}
label2id = {"human": 0, "ai": 1}
```

The label is **authorship**, not truthfulness. The positive class means "AI-generated or AI-rephrased", not "fake". This choice is reflected in the API (`aiProbability`), the verdict wording ("likely AI-written"), and the warning `detector_not_proof`.

The evaluation protocol follows four rules:

1. **Human data must predate ChatGPT.** For Amazon, only reviews dated before 2022-01-01 are used as human. This prevents post-ChatGPT human text from contaminating the negative class.
2. **Split by group, not randomly.** Human reviews are split by `parent_asin`; document chunks are split by `doc_id`. A product or a document never appears in two splits.
3. **Hold out generators and domains.** The test set contains generators and a domain that were never used in training.
4. **Fit thresholds on validation only.** Thresholds and error rates are computed on validation human text; final numbers are reported on test sets.

### 2.2 Part A — short-review detector

#### 2.2.1 Data sources

The review corpus combines human reviews with synthetic AI reviews.

| Role | Source | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Human reviews (main) | **Amazon Reviews 2023** (McAuley Lab), categories `All_Beauty`, `Health_and_Household`, `Amazon_Fashion` | Training/validation/test human class |
| Human reviews (held-out category) | `Appliances` | Unseen-category test (T4) |
| AI reviews (main) | Synthetic reviews generated with several open-weight LLM families via **Ollama** | Training/validation/test AI class |
| AI reviews (optional external tests) | RAID reviews domain; Salminen GPT-2 fake-review dataset; Yelp/OpSpam | External generalisation checks |

**Human filtering.** Reviews are kept only if they are dated before 2022-01-01, contain 15–160 words, are at least 95% ASCII (English-dominant), and are not duplicates. Fields retained: `text`, `rating`, `category`, `parent_asin`, `label=0`, `generator="human"`.

**AI generation.** At least four generators from different model families are used, for example two Llama/Mistral-family models for training and Qwen/Gemma-family models held out for testing. Each generator produces four modes with an equal share:

| Mode | Prompt intent |
|---|---|
| `scratch` | Write a review for a product/category from scratch |
| `rephrase` | Rewrite a human review, preserving meaning and sentiment |
| `casual` | Write like a real shopper typing on a phone, with minor imperfections |
| `short` | Write a very short 15–25 word review |

Generation uses temperature 0.9 and top-p 0.95. Output is cleaned to remove assistant preambles ("Sure", "Here is a review", "As an AI…"), quotes, and refusals. Kept reviews must be 15–160 words. The target is about 3,000 reviews per generator and roughly **12k human + 12k AI** reviews for training/validation/testing, with held-out generators used only for testing.

#### 2.2.2 Splits and leakage controls

- Human split: `GroupShuffleSplit` on `parent_asin`, 70/15/15 train/validation/test within the seen categories. `Appliances` is a separate unseen-category test.
- AI split: generators G1/G2 are split 70/15/15 into train/validation/test; generators G3/G4 (and optional G5) are test-only.
- Evaluation sets:

| Set | Human part | AI part | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 in-distribution | `h_test` | G1/G2 test | Same-generator performance |
| T2 unseen generator | `h_test` | G3/G4/G5 | Generalisation to new LLMs |
| T3 adversarial | `h_test` | `casual` mode from all generators | Informal/phone-style AI text |
| T4 unseen category | `Appliances` | AI for `Appliances` | Cross-category transfer |
| T5 external | RAID/OpSpam human | RAID AI | External check |

**Leakage check.** Two simple baselines must *not* separate the classes:

- logistic regression on review length alone;
- logistic regression on TF-IDF of the first three words.

If either reaches AUC > 0.85, the data is rebalanced (length-matched per 10-word bin) and preambles/emojis/quotes are stripped. This prevents the model from learning an artefact such as "AI reviews are longer" instead of writing style.

#### 2.2.3 Fine-tuning

The shipped review checkpoint is **`microsoft/deberta-v3-base`**, a 12-layer DeBERTaV2 model with 768 hidden size, 12 attention heads, 128,100 vocabulary, and a 2-label classification head. The exported fp32 `model.safetensors` is 737,719,272 bytes (about 184.4M parameters).

The actual training arguments stored in the checkpoint are:

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Base model | `microsoft/deberta-v3-base` |
| Epochs | 2 |
| Learning rate | 1e-5 |
| Train batch size | 16 |
| Gradient accumulation | 2 (effective batch 32) |
| Eval batch size | 64 |
| Weight decay | 0.01 |
| LR schedule | Linear |
| Max gradient norm | 1.0 |
| Precision | fp16 |
| Max sequence length | 256 tokens |
| Best-model metric | AUROC |
| Evaluation/save | Every epoch |
| Seed | 42 |
| Hardware | Google Colab, NVIDIA T4 |

Training uses `Trainer` with `load_best_model_at_end=True` and `metric_for_best_model="auroc"`. The tokenizer is saved with the model. The final artifact is the best AUROC checkpoint, exported as `model.safetensors` with `config.json`, `tokenizer.json`, `tokenizer_config.json`, and `training_args.bin`.

#### 2.2.4 Calibration and deployed behaviour

The training plan defines a richer calibration stack: 22 stylometric features, a logistic-regression feature model, and a stacker that combines the transformer logit with the feature logit. Thresholds are fitted on validation human scores:

```text
tau_high = 98th percentile of human validation scores  (≈ at most 2% human FPR)
tau_low  = 60th percentile of human validation scores  (human-leaning band)
```

The exported review artifact contains the transformer head and `threshold.json`, whose contents are:

```json
{"margin_threshold": 7.05, "model": "microsoft/deberta-v3-base", "max_len": 256}
```

`margin_threshold = 7.05` is a logit-margin threshold. On the sigmoid scale it corresponds to roughly **P(ai) ≈ 0.9991**, which this checkpoint essentially never reaches for ordinary text. It is therefore recorded in metadata but **not used as an operational verdict line**. The deployed service uses provisional probability cutoffs (`low = 0.5`, `high = 0.9`, overridable with `THRESHOLD_LOW`/`THRESHOLD_HIGH`) and neutral sensitivity/specificity values (0.5/0.5) so that the prevalence estimate is not falsely corrected. This is a deliberate, documented deployment simplification: the optional stylometric stacker was not part of the exported checkpoint, so the service exposes the transformer probability directly. The raw score and the warning are always shown to the user.

#### 2.2.5 Verification

The full T1–T5 table from the training plan was not stored with the exported checkpoint. The served model was therefore verified with held-out-style examples and a set of operational checks:

| Input | Model | P(AI) | Displayed verdict | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal AI-style review | review | 0.967 | likely AI | clear separation |
| "As an AI…" style text | review | 0.994 | likely AI | preamble-like text is strongly flagged |
| Casual human review | review | 0.498 | likely human | borderline, correctly not flagged |
| Negative human review | review | 0.356 | likely human | low AI probability |
| Long formal AI paragraph | review | 0.996 | likely AI | near the top of the range |
| Long human paragraph | review | 0.019 | likely human | strong separation |

These are sanity checks, not a substitute for the full unseen-generator benchmark. The important qualitative result is that fp32 preserves separation between obvious AI and obvious human text, while the thresholds remain conservative.

### 2.3 Part B — long-document detector

#### 2.3.1 Data sources

The document model is trained on two complementary sources:

| Source | Content | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **HC3** (`Hello-SimpleAI/HC3`) | Human vs. ChatGPT answers in five domains: `finance`, `medicine`, `open_qa`, `reddit_eli5`, `wiki_csai` | In-domain human/ChatGPT data |
| **RAID** (`liamdugan/raid`) | Multi-generator, multi-domain data; `attack = none`; domains `abstracts`, `books`, `news`, `reddit`, `wiki`, `recipes`; up to 60,000 documents streamed | Generator/domain diversity |

HC3 supplies clean human/ChatGPT pairs but is ChatGPT-only. RAID adds many generators and domains, which is essential for the unseen-generator test. The base checkpoint is **`Hello-SimpleAI/chatgpt-detector-roberta`**, a RoBERTa-base model already trained on HC3 answers; it is fine-tuned rather than trained from scratch.

**Held-out test conditions:**

- generators: `gpt4`, `cohere-chat`, `mpt-chat`;
- domain: `recipes`.

These never appear in training. This is the key difference from a random split: the test set measures transfer, not memorisation.

#### 2.3.2 Chunking and splits

Long documents are split into overlapping word chunks:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Chunk size | 160 words |
| Stride | 120 words (40-word overlap) |
| Minimum chunk | 60 words (first chunk may be ≥30) |
| Max tokens per chunk | 256 |

Chunks inherit the document label, generator, source, and `doc_id`. The split is grouped by `doc_id` (70/15/15), so chunks of one document never cross splits. Training chunks are capped at 20,000 per generator and 25,000 per source, then the AI and human classes are down-sampled to 1:1. With 2,246 optimizer steps per epoch and a batch size of 16, the balanced training set is approximately **35,936 chunks per epoch**.

Evaluation sets:

| Set | Composition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| T1 in-distribution | `test_id` chunks + human test chunks | Same-generator/domain |
| T2 unseen generator | held-out generator chunks + human test | New LLM generalisation |
| T4 unseen domain | `recipes` chunks + human test | New domain generalisation |

Document-level aggregation uses the mean chunk probability and the share of flagged chunks. This avoids letting one extreme chunk decide a 20-page document.

#### 2.3.3 Fine-tuning

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Base model | `Hello-SimpleAI/chatgpt-detector-roberta` (RoBERTa-base) |
| Epochs | 2 |
| Learning rate | 1e-5 |
| Train batch size | 16 |
| Eval batch size | 64 |
| Weight decay | 0.01 |
| Warmup ratio | 0.06 |
| LR schedule | Linear |
| Max gradient norm | 1.0 |
| Precision | fp16 |
| Max sequence length | 256 tokens |
| Best-model metric | AUC |
| Evaluation/save | Every epoch |
| Seed | 42 |
| Hardware | Google Colab, NVIDIA T4 |

The exported model is **checkpoint-4492** (the epoch-2 best), a 12-layer RoBERTa with 768 hidden size, 12 heads, 50,265 vocabulary, and 124.7M parameters. The fp32 `model.safetensors` is 498,612,824 bytes (about 476 MiB).

#### 2.3.4 Evaluation results

The trainer state records the following validation metrics:

| Epoch | Step | Eval loss | Eval accuracy | Eval AUC | Eval F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2,246 | 0.09757 | 0.98284 | 0.98144 | 0.98644 |
| 2 | 4,492 | **0.08540** | **0.98377** | **0.99768** | **0.98716** |

The best checkpoint is `checkpoint-4492` with `best_metric = 0.997680597130675`. The large AUC jump from epoch 1 (0.981) to epoch 2 (0.998) while accuracy/F1 move only slightly indicates that the model was already classifying most chunks correctly after epoch 1 and became much better at ranking the ambiguous ones after epoch 2. This is exactly the behaviour AUC is meant to capture.

These are **chunk-level validation** numbers. The unseen-generator and unseen-domain test results are folded into the deployment metadata:

| Metadata | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `thresholds.low` | 0.00019910391711164266 | 60th percentile of human validation chunks (human-leaning band) |
| `thresholds.high` | 0.9962259531021118 | 98th percentile of human validation chunks (≈2% human FPR) |
| `sensitivity_unseen` | 0.9850839030453697 | TPR on the unseen-generator test |
| `specificity` | 0.9800055284253202 | 1 − FPR on the in-distribution human test |

The high threshold is deliberately conservative: it flags only chunks above 0.9962, so about 2% of human validation chunks would be flagged. The `low` threshold is very small because human chunks cluster near zero; scores between `low` and `high` are shown as uncertain/human-leaning rather than as confident AI.

#### 2.3.5 Error-corrected prevalence for bulk reviews

For a batch of reviews, the raw "flagged share" is not an unbiased estimate of the AI share because the detector has imperfect sensitivity and specificity. The backend applies the Rogan–Gladen correction:

```text
p_hat = (observed_flagged_share + specificity - 1) / (sensitivity + specificity - 1)
```

with a confidence interval derived from the binomial proportion. For the review model, the deployed metadata uses neutral 0.5/0.5 values, so the estimate reduces to the observed share; for the document model the 0.985/0.980 values are available. The UI shows the observed count, the estimated prevalence, and the interval, and labels the result as an estimate.

## 3. How Users Use It

The product has one page with three workflows. All inference happens locally; the browser talks only to the Next.js server, which talks to the local model service.

### 3.1 Check a text

1. Open the app at `http://localhost:3000`.
2. Paste text into the composer (or use a sample chip for a human/AI example).
3. Submit with the button or `Ctrl/Cmd + Enter`.
4. Read the result: verdict stamp, large probability, confidence word, word count, model version, evidence phrases highlighted in the text, style cues when available, and the "estimate, not proof" disclaimer.
5. Use **Copy summary** to put a one-line result on the clipboard, or print the page. The print stylesheet hides controls.

Routing is automatic: text of **160 words or fewer** goes to the review model; longer text goes to the document model. The API also accepts an explicit `model` override for experiments.

### 3.2 Check a document

1. Go to the **Document** tab and drag a file onto the drop zone (or browse).
2. Supported: `.txt`, `.md`, `.docx`, `.pdf` up to **10 MB**. PDFs must contain selectable text; scanned PDFs return `scanned_pdf` because OCR is not supported.
3. The extractor reads the text, trims to 100,000 characters, chunks it, and scores each chunk with the document model.
4. The result shows the document verdict, the probability, a **chunk strip** (each block coloured by chunk probability and flagged state), the flagged share, and the top evidence phrases. The original file text is not returned to the browser, which keeps the response small and the file contents local.
5. A truncation warning appears if the document exceeded the character limit.

### 3.3 Check reviews in bulk

1. Go to the **Reviews** tab.
2. Paste reviews separated by blank lines, or upload a CSV with a text column (and optional `id`/`rating`).
3. The backend normalises text, drops reviews under 15 words, and scores the rest with the review model in batches.
4. The report shows: total/analysed/skipped counts, flagged count, an observed and error-corrected prevalence figure with an interval, a per-review table (probability, verdict, flagged state, word count) that expands to show details, top evidence phrases, and an experimental group-similarity note when several reviews share a sentence structure.
5. Limits: **150 reviews per request**, rate-limited per IP. The UI states the limit before submission.

## 4. Quantization

### 4.1 The concept

Quantization stores numbers with fewer bits. In **post-training dynamic INT8 quantization**, the weights of selected layers (usually `nn.Linear`) are converted from 32-bit floats to 8-bit integers offline. At inference time, activations are quantized dynamically per batch, the integer matrix multiplication runs, and the result is dequantized back to float.

For an affine quantization scheme:

```text
q = round(x / S) + Z
x_hat = S * (q - Z)
```

where `S` is the scale and `Z` is the zero-point. The error is bounded by roughly half a quantization step. The benefit is memory and speed: 4 bytes per weight become 1 byte (a theoretical ~4× reduction), and integer kernels can be faster on CPUs. The cost is a shift in the output distribution, which matters for a detector that displays a probability and applies thresholds.

### 4.2 What we did

The training plans originally proposed ONNX Runtime dynamic quantization (`AutoQuantizationConfig.avx2`, static=False, per-channel=False). During implementation the team moved to native PyTorch inference for the exported `safetensors` checkpoints, so the same idea was applied with PyTorch:

```python
import torch
model_q8 = torch.ao.quantization.quantize_dynamic(
    model, {torch.nn.Linear}, dtype=torch.qint8
)
model_q8.eval()
```

The service exposes a `QUANTIZE` environment variable (`0` by default). When enabled, each model is quantized after loading. The service always logs whether quantization was applied and reports `"quantized": true/false` in `/health` and `/meta/<model>`.

For the document model, the training notebook implements an explicit acceptance test on 200 validation chunks:

```text
p_fp32 = predict_proba_cpu(texts, model_fp32)
p_q8   = predict_proba_cpu(texts, model_q8)
auc_drop = auc_fp32 - auc_q8

if auc_drop > 0.005:
    ship fp32 only
else:
    save model_q8.pt and ship fp32 + INT8
```

This is the right test for a ranking model: individual probabilities may shift, but the ranking (AUC) must survive. The notebook also prints the maximum and mean absolute probability difference for transparency.

### 4.3 Results and decision

**Review model — negative result.** Dynamic INT8 was not usable. After quantization, both classes collapsed to a narrow band around **0.73**: obvious AI text and obvious human text were no longer separated. The fp32 model gives 0.967/0.996 for AI and 0.019–0.575 for human; INT8 erased that range. Because the product's thresholds and evidence depend on separation, INT8 was rejected for the review model.

**Document model — gated acceptance.** The acceptance rule keeps INT8 only if the AUC drop is at most 0.005. The shipped document folder does **not** contain a `model_q8.pt` (the archive contains the q8 config/tokenizer files but not quantized weights), so the deployed document model is **fp32**. The service default is `QUANTIZE=0` for both models.

**Final deployment:** both models run in fp32. The cost is memory (737.7 MB + 498.6 MB of weights) and some CPU time, but the benefit is preserved probability calibration and separation. On the test machine (8 vCPU), the document model processes a long chunk in about **197 ms**, and a 223–284-word document through the API in **4.3–4.7 s**; the review model returns two long texts in **1.2 s**. These times are acceptable for the 40-second single-request target.

### 4.4 Lessons

- INT8 is not automatically safe for a **probability-emitting** detector. A model can keep a decent AUC on a balanced benchmark yet collapse the calibrated probabilities that the UI and thresholds rely on.
- An AUC-gated acceptance test is better than a max-absolute-difference test alone, but it is still not sufficient for calibrated probabilities. A future version should also check **expected calibration error (ECE)** and the positive/negative score distributions at the chosen thresholds.
- Quantization must be evaluated on the same preprocessing and chunking used in production, not on a different max length or tokenizer.
- For CPU deployment, ONNX Runtime with static quantization, per-channel scales, and a calibration set is the next thing to try; quantized-aware training (QAT) is the fallback if post-training quantization remains unstable.
- The 4× memory reduction is attractive (about 185 MB and 125 MB theoretical for the two models), but not at the cost of a detector that cannot tell AI from human.

A note on the ONNX path: the initial design used Transformers.js/ONNX Runtime in Node. The team replaced the hot path with a local PyTorch service because the exported checkpoints are native `safetensors` and the PyTorch service gave better control over batching, thresholds, and quantization experiments. The Next.js layer still owns validation, extraction, limits, and presentation; it does not load model weights.

### 5.2 Request flows

```text
Browser (Next.js / React)
   │  fetch JSON or multipart
   ▼
Next.js Route Handlers  (/api/detect/text, /file, /reviews, /api/health)
   │  Zod validation, rate limit, typed errors
   ▼
Pipelines
   ├─ detectText: normalise → count words → route review/document
   ├─ detectFile: txt/md/docx/pdf extractor → detectText(..., "document")
   └─ detectReviews: normalise → drop <15 words → batch score → report
   │  HTTP (ML_SERVICE_URL, ML_TIMEOUT_MS)
   ▼
FastAPI service (ml/app.py)
   │  per-model tokenizer + model + lock + batch
   ▼
PyTorch fp32: DeBERTa-v3 (review) | RoBERTa (document)
```

**Text flow.** `POST /api/detect/text` accepts `{ text, model? }`. The pipeline normalises curly quotes, counts words, adds warnings (`short_text` under 40 words, `detector_not_proof` always), chooses the model (≤160 words → review, >160 → document), checks readiness, scores, computes the verdict/confidence, and (for review) adds evidence and style cues.

**File flow.** `POST /api/detect/file` accepts a multipart `file`. The registry picks an extractor by extension/MIME, joins extracted units, truncates to 100,000 characters, forces the document model, and attaches `source: { fileName, chars }`. PDFs with fewer than 200 non-space characters are rejected as `scanned_pdf`.

**Reviews flow.** `POST /api/detect/reviews` accepts JSON `{ reviews: [{ id?, text, rating? }] }` or a multipart CSV. Reviews shorter than 15 words are skipped. The remaining texts are scored in batches, converted to per-review verdicts, and aggregated into flagged count, prevalence (observed + corrected + interval), top evidence (up to 5 phrases), and group signals.

**Health flow.** `GET /api/health` asks the service for both models' metadata and readiness. The response is:

```json
{
  "ok": true,
  "models": { "review": "review-v2", "document": "document-v1" },
  "available": true,
  "ready": { "review": true, "document": true }
}
```

### 5.3 Limits, errors, and privacy

| Control | Value |
|---|---|
| Max text | 100,000 characters |
| Max file | 10 MB |
| Max reviews per request | 150 |
| Min review length for bulk analysis | 15 words |
| Review/document routing boundary | 160 words |
| Rate limits | text 30/min, file 10/min, reviews 20/min per IP |
| Model timeout | 90 s (configurable) |

Errors use a stable shape `{ error: { code, message } }` with codes such as `empty_text` (422), `too_large` (413), `unsupported_type` (415), `scanned_pdf` (422), `rate_limited` (429), `bad_request` (400), `model_unavailable` (503), and `internal` (500). The UI maps these to readable messages.

**Privacy.** There is no database and no request-body logging. Text is processed in memory and discarded. The model service binds to `127.0.0.1` by default. Uploaded file text is not returned to the browser; only scores and evidence offsets/phrases are returned. The API always includes the `detector_not_proof` warning, and the Method page states the limits.

### 6.4 What we can and cannot claim

**We can claim:** the two models separate obvious AI and human text in fp32; the document model has strong validation AUC and calibrated metadata; the system is fast enough for interactive use; the product works end-to-end with files and bulk reviews; the quantization study is reproducible.

**We cannot claim:** that the output proves a text is fake; that the models are unbiased across dialects, genres, or non-native English; that the review model's full unseen-generator benchmark is included in the exported artifact; that INT8 is safe for these models; or that a 2% human false-positive rate is acceptable for every deployment. Those are limitations, not hidden assumptions.

## 7. Limitations and Ethics

1. **Authorship is not intent.** "AI-written" does not mean "fake" or "harmful". A truthful review can be AI-assisted, and a human can write a deceptive review. The UI says this explicitly.
2. **English-only training.** Both datasets are English-dominant. Performance on code-mixed, transliterated, or low-resource text is unknown. Work such as [1] shows that language-specific models are needed.
3. **Domain shift.** A model trained on Amazon/HC3/RAID may not transfer to legal, medical, or highly technical writing. The held-out domain test is a start, not a guarantee.
4. **Generator drift.** New models appear continuously. The unseen-generator test reduces overfitting to known LLMs, but it cannot anticipate future architectures. Periodic re-training and re-calibration are required.
5. **Short text is unreliable.** Under about 40 words, the evidence is weak. The UI warns about this.
6. **False positives hurt real people.** A 2% human FPR means 2 in 100 human texts could be flagged at the high threshold. In high-stakes settings, the tool must be advisory and human-reviewed.
7. **Calibration mismatch in the review deployment.** The exported review artifact does not include the stylometric stacker, and the `margin_threshold` is not operationally usable. The provisional 0.5/0.9 cutoffs are honest but not the final word; a full calibration pass is future work.
8. **Quantization is not free.** The review INT8 result is a warning: always evaluate the full score distribution and ECE, not only accuracy or AUC.
9. **Privacy has limits.** Local processing is good, but the service still receives text over localhost; in a shared deployment, logs and access controls must be configured. The current code avoids body logging but does not encrypt memory.
10. **No OCR.** Scanned PDFs are rejected rather than silently producing empty results.

---

## 8. Future Work

- **Complete the review benchmark.** Export the T1–T5 table (in-distribution, unseen generator, adversarial casual, unseen category, external) and fit the full stylometric + stacker calibration on validation data.
- **Recalibrate the review thresholds.** Replace the provisional 0.5/0.9 cutoffs with validation-derived quantiles and report ECE alongside AUC.
- **ONNX Runtime + static quantization.** Try per-channel static INT8 with a calibration set; compare latency, memory, AUC, and ECE against fp32.
- **Quantization-aware training (QAT)** for the review model if post-training INT8 remains unstable.
- **More generators and languages.** Add newer open-weight and commercial models to the held-out set; extend to code-mixed and low-resource languages.
- **Document-level aggregation research.** Compare mean probability, flagged share, top-k chunks, and learned aggregation on long documents.
- **Better evidence.** Move from occlusion to attention/attribution methods and evaluate whether the highlighted phrases actually help users.
- **Deployment hardening.** Add authentication, structured audit logs without text, a job queue for very large batches, and a Docker image.
- **User studies.** Measure whether the verdict, probability, and evidence are understood and whether the "estimate, not proof" framing reduces over-trust.

---

## 9. Conclusion

txt turns a research idea into a working, local-first tool. The gap is not a missing percentage point on a benchmark; it is the missing bridge between (a) AI-authorship detection, (b) generalisation to unseen generators and domains, (c) long-form documents, and (d) a calibrated, explainable, privacy-preserving product. The project trains two transformers with grouped splits and held-out conditions, evaluates them honestly, documents a failed INT8 attempt and a gated document acceptance rule, and ships a Next.js + FastAPI system that a user can run on a laptop. The most important next step is to complete the review model's unseen-generator benchmark and replace the provisional review thresholds with a fully calibrated stacker, so that the review path is as rigorously calibrated as the document path.
